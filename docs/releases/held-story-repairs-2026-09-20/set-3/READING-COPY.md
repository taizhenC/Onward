### Passage 1 — scene

A fifty-one-year-old organizer went onto the gathering ground with a colleague. The colleague remembered finding no crowd there. They had arrived early.

### Passage 2 — dark moment

He had planned a nonviolent gathering with workers from several organizations. But the expected people were not yet in front of him. The ground was empty.

### Passage 3 — response

He asked his colleague whether anybody was coming to the march. That is the question the colleague remembered. He said it aloud.

### Passage 4 — struggle

The previous night had brought a different problem. He had called a young speaker to a hotel meeting about objections to the speech. Representatives of the organizations argued about it. They disagreed over support for a proposed civil-rights law.

The meeting did not settle every objection. Disagreement continued the next day where the speeches were to be given. There was still a speech to agree on.

### Passage 5 — turning point

On the gathering ground, young marchers appeared just after his question. In his colleague's memory, more people followed in a steady flow. The crowd was arriving.

### Passage 6 — became

Later that day, he addressed the crowd himself. He called for a civil-rights law and asked the people to respond. He named housing, schools, employment and voting rights.

Weeks later, he wrote about what the march had achieved and what remained undone. Racist violence had killed four children in a church. He said the organizers should have prepared their next steps. The gathering had not ended their work.

### Passage 7 — bridge

His name was Bayard Rustin.
