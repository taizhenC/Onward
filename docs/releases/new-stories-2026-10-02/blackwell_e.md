## Research and scope

A woman seeking a complete medical education met evasive refusals, sent applications to smaller schools, and accepted an invitation to join a class.

College admission brought a place to study, but exclusion continued in demonstrations and hospital training; the diploma marked a first step in learning.

## Full reading copy

### 1. scene

She sat with a doctor, asking whether she could enter medical classes. She was twenty-six. She explained what she wanted to do. He sat without speaking for several minutes. She had come to him feeling gloomy. Other doctors had already opposed her entering their schools.

She had studied through private lectures and visits to patients. She had used a doctor's library. What she still needed was a full course at a medical college. Being a woman had kept that course closed to her. She asked him for some encouragement.

### 2. dark_moment

The doctor called her request a new subject. He would not tell her whether he supported it. He said her words had set him thinking. He could not explain his opinion. She asked whether his answer meant he opposed her. He said he had expressed no opinion either way. He repeated that he could not describe how his mind was working.

She asked whether she should call on the other professors at his college. He would not take responsibility for that advice. She asked whether he could let her attend his lectures. Again, he said he had expressed no opinion. There was no admission and no direction to another professor. She got up in despair and left.

### 3. response

Later, she obtained a list of smaller medical schools. She examined their printed descriptions of the courses they offered. These schools lay beyond the cities where her applications had failed. She looked for a full course of instruction under able professors. She selected twelve schools that seemed promising.

She sent each an application asking to be admitted. She could examine the courses and send her request. She could not yet enter any of those classes. The applications were now with the schools. She waited for their answers.

### 4. struggle

No answer came for some time. The winter courses were approaching. She waited with growing anxiety about whether she would get a place. Her earlier applications to city schools had failed. The private lessons and library she had used could not replace their full course.

One doctor had allowed her to visit his patients and attend his lectures. That access had given her real opportunities to study. It had not brought admission to a medical college. Now she was trying schools she had chosen from printed descriptions. The replies she needed had still not arrived.

### 5. turning_point

At last, a letter arrived from a college dean. The faculty had put her application before the medical students. The students had met and answered it themselves. Their reply was enclosed with the dean's letter. Every student had voted to invite her into the class.

They had written that education should be open equally to women and men. They had made a promise about how they would treat her. None of their conduct should make her regret coming to their school. Their invitation gave her the college entry she had been seeking. The faculty had met the night before the students cast their votes. The dean wished her success, though some people might consider her undertaking bold. She felt relief and gratitude. She accepted at once and prepared to travel.

### 6. became

She enrolled and decorated her new room with reminders of absent friends. She studied anatomy beside four other students and worked late at night. Her classmates kept a place for her in the lecture rooms. Some demonstrations were still closed to her. She wrote to the professor, asking to be treated as a student. The class supported her request.

During later hospital study, some doctors stopped writing information on the patients' cards. She had to study without that help. She returned to her college, completed her examinations, and received her degree. After the other graduates, she was called alone to the platform. The president gave her the diploma. She still needed more experience before taking on the responsibilities of medical practice. There was more study ahead.

### 7. bridge

Her name was Elizabeth Blackwell.

She worked as a doctor and helped create a medical school where women could receive the training the profession required.

Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You do not have to know how it ends to keep going. You are allowed to want a place.
