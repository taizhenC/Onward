## Reading copy

### scene

He entered the house with his money in his hand. He was about twenty-one. The man who owned him was having breakfast with the vessel captain. The captain had told him to come that morning. He had asked how to offer his savings for his freedom. Now both men were before him.

The price had been agreed. He had saved more than that amount. He bowed and asked the owner to keep the promise. He had brought the money, but he was afraid. He was asking for his freedom from that man. The answer had not yet been given.

### dark_moment

The owner pulled back from his request. His heart sank. The man asked whether he really had enough money. Then he asked where it had come from. The money was in his hand. The promise was still in the power of the other man.

He had earned enough to meet the price. That did not itself make him free. He had to ask the man who owned him to accept it. The owner had not expected him to save so much so quickly. He said he would not have promised freedom if he had known. The savings had reached their goal. The man who had set that goal was backing away.

### response

He answered the question about his money. He told the owner how he had earned it. The captain supported his answer. He said the money had been earned honestly and with work. He said the young man had been careful.

The owner said he had saved faster than the owner himself. The earlier promise now seemed to trouble him. The captain was there as a witness to the earnings. The young sailor had brought both the money and an account of how he got it. He had spoken for his request. The owner still had to agree.

### struggle

Before this breakfast, the money had come through voyages and trading. Earlier that year he had hoped a return home would let him sell his goods. The owner sent the vessel elsewhere. He was disappointed. At the new stop, the vessel took on other people held in slavery. At that stop he could not put all his money into goods at a good price. He carried some of it away unspent.

Later, the captain promised him extra money for helping care for a dying man. The captain expected to receive property the man owned. They opened the trunks, expecting something valuable inside. They found only a little money. The hoped-for extra payment failed. After another return, he sold his own goods and counted what he had. He had enough to ask for the promised release.

### turning_point

At the breakfast, the captain urged the owner to take the money. He reminded him of the value of the work the sailor had done. The owner had received that work and its profits. Now he could receive the original price as well. The captain pressed him to keep the promise.

The owner agreed and took the money. He told the young man to have a release document drawn up. The fear he had brought into the house turned to joy. He bowed and cried. He thanked the men before him. Then he left the room to get the paper. The request had been accepted. The release still needed to be written and signed.

### became

He paid to have the release written, then returned for a signature from the owner. The man signed it that day. Before night, he was no longer his property. He remained with the vessel as a sailor earning wages. He also wanted to leave for another country.

On the next voyage, after a quarrel, a merchant threatened to have him beaten. He was afraid and went into hiding after others urged him to do so. Friends insisted that the warrant be taken back before he returned to the vessel. He paid the expenses and went back to his work. The release was real. It had not removed the threats around him.

### bridge

His name was Olaudah Equiano.

He wrote about his enslavement and freedom and argued against slavery.

Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You do not have to know how it ends to keep going. You are allowed to be uncertain.

## Avoid rules

- Do not say savings alone secured freedom; the owner controlled release and the captain intervened.
- Do not describe the owner as a liberator free of participation in enslavement.
- Do not relabel chapter VI incidents as 1765; its ending explicitly dates them 1764.
- Do not collapse July 10 signature and July 11 registration.
- Do not confuse 40 pounds sterling with 70 pounds island currency.
- Do not present self-reported 1745 birth or an African birthplace as an independently resolved fact.
- Do not promise that work or saving can produce freedom under enslavement.
- Do not omit the continued racial threats after legal manumission.
