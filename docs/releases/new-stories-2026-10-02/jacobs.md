## Reading copy

### scene

She lay on a bed beneath a low, sloping roof. She was in her early twenties. She had been hidden in a space at her grandmother’s house. She could not stand there. When she turned on the bed, her body struck the roof. Light and air could not reach her through the boards.

She had escaped from the people who held her in slavery. The hiding place kept her close to her family. Relatives passed food to her through an opening they could close. They could come to speak with her when darkness made it safe. Below her, the house still held voices she knew.

### dark_moment

She heard her children below and began to cry. Their voices brought joy and sadness together. She wanted to speak to them. She wanted to see their faces. She could find no crack in the boards to look through. The darkness stayed around her.

She sat or lay with her body cramped beneath the roof. She needed air even more than light. No change in the room marked the coming of morning. The sounds outside told her when it had arrived. Her children were close enough for her to hear. There was no opening through which she could watch them. She was eager for even a glimpse of their faces. She could hear them, and could not call back.

### response

While crawling for exercise she hit her head against something hard. It was a small tool for boring holes. Her uncle had left it when he made the concealed door. She felt around for the side that faced the street. She waited until evening to work. She feared making a sound that someone could notice in daylight.

She bored holes through the wood, then joined the spaces between them. The opening was small. She sat beside it late into the night. A little air came through. In the morning she watched for her children. The first person she saw was the man pursuing her. The sight frightened her. Later, her children came into view.

### struggle

The small opening did not end her confinement. Nearly seven years passed with her hidden beneath the roof. At times she felt too dull and tired to do anything. At other times she was impatient for sunshine and open air. The roof began to fail. Her uncle feared that taking off the covering would expose her. Rain drenched her clothes and bedding and made her stiff limbs ache.

A friend found a way for her to leave on a vessel. After news of a fugitive being killed, her grandmother begged her to stay. She gave up that chance. She offered her place to another woman in hiding. Then a visitor entered the room where she was speaking with her grandmother. She crouched behind a barrel and feared she had been seen. Her family urged her to leave.

### turning_point

The captain met her as she entered the vessel. He showed her into a small cabin. There sat the woman who had taken the passage first offered to her. The woman stared in surprise. They held each other, and she began to sob. The captain heard them.

He came to remind them that noise could put them all in danger. The women talked quietly in the little cabin. The vessel moved away slowly. While they remained near the town, she feared people coming aboard to take her back. She wondered whether the captain might surrender her for money. Her companion told her the crew had treated her kindly. The captain invited them onto the deck and found them seats. She went with him into the air.

### became

Near the end of the voyage, she called her companion onto the deck at dawn. They watched the sun rise over the water. A city they did not know lay ahead. Both women had tears in their eyes. They thought they were safe from capture. They had also left people they loved behind.

After landing, a minister and his wife gave her a welcome and a place to rest. Other people offered to help with her plans. Travel onward brought a different reminder of what had not changed. She was refused a seat in the better cars because she was Black. The ride was crowded and unpleasant. She had escaped, but she had not been legally freed. She had come out from under the roof. She was still trying to reach her children.

### bridge

Her name was Harriet Ann Jacobs.

She wrote an account of her enslavement and escape.

Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You do not have to know how it ends to keep going. You are allowed to not be ready yet.
