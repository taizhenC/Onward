## Research and scope

A six-year-old child copied finger movements before she understood what words meant, then connected a word with the thing she was touching.

Her asking and learning grew through sustained teaching, but using sentences and searching for hidden objects still took practice.

## Full reading copy

### 1. scene

She waited on the porch with her fingers resting against leaves and blossoms. She was six. Sunlight fell on her upturned face. She could not see the people moving about inside the house. She could not hear what they said. Her mother's signs suggested that something unusual was happening.

She expected something unusual to happen, without knowing what it would be. The leaves and flowers were familiar beneath her fingers. She went to the door and waited on the steps. The house was busy around her. She waited there on the steps.

### 2. dark_moment

Before that afternoon, trying to speak had made her angry and worn out. She had touched people's lips as they talked to one another. Their mouths moved. She could not understand the conversation. She tried moving her own lips. She made signs with her hands. The effort did not give her the speech she was trying to copy.

Her mother could understand many of the signs she already used. Pulling someone could mean that they should come with her. Pushing could mean that they should go. But moving her lips had failed. She had kicked and screamed until she was exhausted. In the weeks before this afternoon, anger had given way to weariness.

### 3. response

The next morning, the new teacher gave her a doll. After she had played with it, the teacher made movements inside her hand. She formed the letters of the doll's name slowly. The girl was interested in what the fingers were doing. She tried to make the same movements herself.

When she got them right, she felt pleasure and pride. She ran downstairs to her mother. She held up her hand and showed her the movements. She did not yet know that she had made a word. She was copying the fingers she had felt.

### 4. struggle

Over the following weeks, she learned to form many more finger words. She could copy names for small objects and movements such as sitting or walking. She still did not understand that things had names. The words for a cup and its milk kept getting confused. She made the sign for drinking when she formed either word.

The days also held digging, beads, dolls, and work with yarn. She learned to make a washcloth for her mother. The teacher formed words in her hand through their daily activities. The girl's fingers could repeat a growing number of them. The meaning of all that spelling had still not become clear.

### 5. turning_point

One morning, while washing, she asked for the name of the water. The teacher formed the word in her hand. After breakfast, they went outside to the water spout. The girl held her cup beneath it. Cold water flowed into the cup and over her hand. In her free hand, the teacher formed the same word again.

She dropped the cup and stood still. The movement in one hand named the cool thing flowing over the other. She began forming the water word herself, again and again. Then she reached down to the ground and asked for its name. She pointed to the spout and the frame beside it. She asked for the teacher's name. On the way back, she asked about the objects she touched.

### 6. became

In the days that followed, she wanted names for things beyond the house. She asked her friends to make letters and tried to teach them herself. When she learned a word, it could take the place of an old sign. A single word and a gesture could now carry a whole request. She did not yet form full sentences herself.

A game with hidden objects still gave her trouble. She searched places where a ball or spool could not fit. At first, she gave up quickly. With practice, she could keep searching longer. The teacher still had to supply words and their meanings. She kept asking for more names.

### 7. bridge

Her name was Helen Adams Keller.

She became a writer whose work helped press for the rights of people with disabilities.

Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You do not have to know how it ends to keep going. You are allowed to need more time.
