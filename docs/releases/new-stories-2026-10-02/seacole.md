## Reading copy

### 1. scene

She waited in a great hall while people passed in and out. She was in her late forties.

She had looked after sick people in more than one country. Now she wanted to go where soldiers were lying wounded and ill. She had brought references from people who knew her work.

The servants wanted her to leave. She stayed through their excuses and their irritation, waiting for someone who could hear her offer. This was another application after the offices had sent her from one department to another.

Then a message reached her. All the nursing places had been filled.

### 2. dark_moment

She tried another nursing organizer and received the same answer. At last she asked a relief fund for passage to the soldiers. Once there, she hoped some work would turn up. That request failed too.

She stood in the street on a cold evening. The twilight was deepening, and the people around her were making their way home. She had wanted to put her experience to use. Instead, she was still here.

She began to wonder whether the colour of her skin lay behind the refusals. She could not see into the minds of the people who had answered her. What she had was the suspicion, and the pain of feeling her motives doubted.

Tears ran down her face. She stopped, looked up through the dark clouds, and prayed aloud for help. There in the thinning street, she cried.

### 3. response

The next morning, after a night of sleep, she felt her determination return. She would go herself, with her own money and on her own responsibility. People at the destination had known her work before. She hoped they would welcome her.

She planned a hotel where sick and recovering officers could find meals and somewhere comfortable to stay. She had cards printed announcing her passage and what she intended to provide. The cards went ahead to people she knew.

An acquaintance was travelling there on shipping business. They agreed that they might open a store together alongside the hotel. She had a partner.

### 4. struggle

She put much of her limited money into medicines, choosing them with help from a medical friend. With the rest, she bought comforts she thought would be hard to find far from home. Then she boarded the ship.

On the way, officers she had cared for before recognized her. They were glad to see her, but warned her about the place ahead. She would struggle even to find a roof. She continued.

Letters from her partner brought worse accounts of the conditions. Moving her goods to the next ship meant strong currents and a fierce wind. She clambered aboard drenched, after an anxious struggle. When she finally arrived, there were no warehouses for their stores. They stacked the goods on shore.

### 5. turning_point

On her first visit to the wharf where wounded men were being moved onto ships, a group had just arrived. One man lay on a pallet, groaning. She ran to him and eased the stiff dressings.

Her fingers moved through work she already knew. His groans diminished to an uneasy mutter. She bent down and brought tea to his lips. He touched her hand and held it.

She stayed beside the man while he continued to hold her hand. A surgeon came towards them. He shook her hand, thanked her for looking after his patient, and welcomed her there.

The man was still wounded. The work of moving the sick onto the transports was still waiting. The surgeon was glad to have her help with it. Her hand had been held by a patient; now another hand welcomed her.

### 6. became

For six weeks she spent her days on shore and slept aboard a ship at night. Sheets of rough tarpaulin sheltered the stores from rain and wind. Under them she received visitors and sold goods.

Her main work was helping the doctors move sick and wounded men from mules and ambulances into the hospital transports. She kept that work ahead of the business. The wharf became a place she knew through repeated days of attending to the people brought there.

She had arrived with supplies for a venture and a wish to help. Now her days held both the stores and the wounded. At night she returned to the ship.

### 7. bridge

Her name was Mary Seacole.

She was a Jamaican nurse and businesswoman remembered for her work during the Crimean War.

Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You are allowed to feel disappointed.
