# Mary Somerville: a mathematical manuscript under review

Research completed October 3, 2026. This is a source-grounded authoring proposal, not a publication approval.

## Episode and sources opened

The episode begins with the March 27, 1827 request to explain Laplace's mathematical astronomy and ends with the publication and early reception of *Mechanism of the Heavens* in 1831. Mary Somerville was born December 26, 1780; she was forty-six at the request and fifty before December 26, 1831. The narrative must present an experienced, self-taught mathematical reader, not a novice starting mathematics in middle age. [Personal Recollections, Chapter I, p. 8; Chapter XI, pp. 161–170](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27747/27747-h/27747-h.htm).

The main source actually opened is the **1874** fifth-thousand edition of *Personal Recollections, from Early Life to Old Age, of Mary Somerville*, edited by her daughter Martha. Its introduction says the recollections were noted during her mother's final years. Later remembered feelings, dialogue, routines, and assessments therefore receive `probable` confidence. The book also reproduces correspondence: the dated request, the initial undated Herschel review letter, the February 23, 1830 revision letter, and the November 2, 1831 Whewell letter are contemporary documents reproduced by a later editor. Their texts are document evidence; editorial dating or claims about a letter's emotional effect are kept distinct. [Introduction, pp. 1–3; Chapter XI, pp. 161–170](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27747/27747-h/27747-h.htm).

The college that holds her family papers confirms the translation and **1831** publication, and identifies the papers as a collection held for the college by the Bodleian. These are collection-owner sources, used for bibliographic corroboration rather than invented scene particulars. [Somerville College Library, Mary Somerville Collections](https://library.some.ox.ac.uk/2025/06/06/mary-somerville-collections/), [Mary Somerville Papers](https://library.some.ox.ac.uk/special-collections/mary-somerville-papers/).

Her own separately printed *Preliminary Dissertation* was opened, including its title page and initial definition of physical astronomy. It supports the plain bridge description of a writer explaining the mathematics of planetary motion. The opened copy is the **1832 Philadelphia printing**, not proof of the first book's precise publication day. [A Preliminary Dissertation on the Mechanism of the Heavens, title page and opening paragraphs](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/67386/67386-h/67386-h.htm).

## Four supported moments

1. **Scene: a visitor urging an attempt at her home.** A few days after the dated request, Brougham came to Chelsea; her husband joined in urging her to try. She explained the mathematical prerequisites, the need for proofs, and the missing diagrams. The source supplies the visit and speech, but no chair, posture, table, weather, visitor's expression, or exact room. The draft may say “at home,” not invent those particulars. [Chapter XI, pp. 162–163, “This letter surprised me” through the secrecy condition](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27747/27747-h/27747-h.htm).
2. **Dark moment: doubt expressed in the same conversation.** She thought the request overestimated her knowledge and that her self-acquired knowledge was inferior to university-trained men's knowledge. She explicitly said she feared being incapable. Her view is an attested assessment, not a fact that women or self-taught mathematicians were incapable. No shame, trembling, tears, sleeplessness, humiliation by the visitors, or earlier failed book is supplied. [Chapter XI, p. 162](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27747/27747-h/27747-h.htm).
3. **Response: a conditional agreement in that conversation.** She said she would do her best on condition of secrecy and destruction of a failed manuscript. Agreement is the small documented act. The draft must not place an immediate first page on a table, invent a new confidence, or imply that the manuscript was actually burned. [Chapter XI, pp. 162–163](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27747/27747-h/27747-h.htm).
4. **Turn: the initial manuscript-review letter and her preserved copy.** She finished and sent the manuscript for examination, stayed very nervous while it was examined, and was surprised at how few errors Herschel found. The first reproduced review letter praises the work but marks an obscure explanation and a small point on page six. She says this letter made her happy and proud and that she preserved it. This supports a letter-centered turn with corrections still present. The source does not date this first letter, locate its reading, or say that every recommended revision was made. [Chapter XI, pp. 165–166](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27747/27747-h/27747-h.htm).

## Struggle and bounded outcome

Her writing had many interruptions. Her small room lacked a fireplace, forcing winter writing into the drawing-room; visitors could arrive during difficult problems. She hid papers when the bell announced a visitor to preserve secrecy, and learned to stop and resume her work. The narrative uses this documented recurring difficulty in the compression passage, not as an invented single dated interruption. Her daughter remembers teaching and mathematical work sharing a room, with both patience and occasional impatience. These recollections do not support a saintly, uninterrupted genius. [Chapter XI, pp. 163–165](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27747/27747-h/27747-h.htm).

The February 23, 1830 letter offers a specific technical shortcoming: the first part passed over difficulties beginners could not overcome and needed more clarity, fullness, and space. This is a real deficiency at personal scale. The draft preserves the fact of further criticism after initial encouraging review; it does not convert the letters into proof of one instantaneous rescue or claim a documented revision action that the source does not give. [Chapter XI, pp. 167–169, Herschel letter dated February 23, 1830](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27747/27747-h/27747-h.htm).

The 1831 book existed and was sent to university readers. Whewell's November 2, 1831 letter thanks her for copies and says young mathematicians should have access when able to read it. Her own later account says only the preface was intelligible to the general reader, an assessment of the book's content. The separate printing of that preface follows the February 14, 1832 Peacock letter and is outside this episode; it was removed from the revised narrative. Publication did not make the entire mathematical book accessible to novices. The episode ends in 1831, without later medals or a lifetime achievement list. [Chapter XI, pp. 169–172](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27747/27747-h/27747-h.htm).

## Uncertainties and exclusions

- The initial review letter is undated in the opened text. Its placement and her prose establish sequence, not a precise reading date.
- The college's popular biography says the first book appeared when she was fifty-one. That conflicts with a December 26, 1780 birth and the contemporary November 2, 1831 acknowledgment. The draft uses the dated primary correspondence and fifty, and does not cite the fifty-one statement as evidence. [College biography](https://www.some.ox.ac.uk/eminent/mary-somerville/), [birth and 1831 letter](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27747/27747-h/27747-h.htm).
- “Translation” is only a shorthand: her account discusses additional proofs and diagrams. The stage should say explaining mathematical astronomy and writing a manuscript, not a line-by-line translation.
- The publisher's risk is recalled in a later chapter (p. 203), but is unnecessary for the drama and not used to invent a publication contract scene.
- Her later loss of her husband on June 26, 1860 is biographical identity context, not part of this episode or a reason for writing the book.
