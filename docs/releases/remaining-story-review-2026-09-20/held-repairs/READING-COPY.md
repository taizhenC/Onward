## 1. Scene

She was thirty-one when a publisher's letter arrived.

It concerned the novel she had sent out again after other publishers refused it. She had asked this firm whether it could publish the book soon. Now its answer had come.

## 2. Dark moment

She expected a refusal. In her recollection, it was a bleak expectation.

She imagined only a couple of lines saying that the publisher did not want the book. Her hope for this attempt had been small.

## 3. Response

She opened the envelope. Inside was a letter of two pages. She took it out and read. She remembered trembling as she read it.

This answer was longer than the dismissal she had expected. It was still a refusal.

## 4. Struggle

Before this reply, she had already spent a long time trying to reach readers. The poems she published with her sisters had sold only two copies in a year. The sisters had sent their novels to one publisher after another. Her sisters found a publisher, though on poor terms. Her own book remained unwanted. She remembered despair beginning to reach her.

She sent that manuscript to another firm and asked for its judgment. More than two weeks passed without an answer. She wrote again to ask whether the parcel had arrived and whether they could publish it. She enclosed a cover addressed for their reply. The new reply still did not accept that book.

## 5. Turning point

The publisher's letter explained why it could not take this manuscript. But it also gave careful attention to the writing itself. She read an assessment of its strengths and weaknesses. In her memory, that consideration cheered her.

The writer offered to consider a longer novel from the same author. She already had another book nearly finished. There was an invitation to answer.

## 6. Became

She replied that she had another novel almost ready. She still proposed publishing the first book, followed by the second. She asked the publisher to consider that plan. Later that month, she finished the new manuscript and sent it. She could not prepay the delivery charge where she left the parcel. She asked for the cost so she could repay it.

That autumn, six printed copies reached her. She wrote to thank the publisher. She liked the paper, the clear print and the binding. Then she waited for the judgments of the press and the public.

Some early notices were cool. She worried that more criticism could hurt demand. Getting into print had not ended her concern about readers. The new book was in print.

## 7. Bridge

Her name was Charlotte Brontë.

She wrote Jane Eyre.

Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You are allowed to care about work that has not found its readers. You do not have to call it worthless.
