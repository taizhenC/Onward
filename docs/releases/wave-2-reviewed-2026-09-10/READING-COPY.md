### 1. scene

He remembered starting school among boys much younger than himself. He was about seventeen. Grammar, geography, and mathematics were new work, arriving together. He wanted to learn them. The lessons seemed more than he could take in. His attempt to write for the theatre had been rejected.

The people reading his play had offered schooling instead of a place onstage. People had arranged money and a place for him at this school. His progress mattered to people outside the room. He kept his attention on the headmaster. A question came to him. He gave the wrong answer.

### 2. dark moment

The headmaster called him stupid. He believed the man. He did not hear an error in a lesson being corrected. He heard a judgment about himself. The people helping him expected him to learn. He feared he did not deserve what they had done.

The help had opened a place at school. Now he doubted whether he belonged in it.

### 3. response

He had told the man arranging his support about his anxiety. The man had consoled him. Four years into school, he wrote directly to the headmaster. He said he could not find the courage to speak in person. He asked the man to read the whole letter. He still believed the headmaster meant to help him. He described how fear confused his answers. He said that he had worked, but had lost hope and calm.

He wanted to go on studying. He feared facing the people who had expected more from him. If any hope remained, he asked the man not to lose patience. He asked for advice and help. At the end, he worried that he had written too much. He asked the headmaster not to be angry.

### 4. struggle

He was now living in the headmaster's house as well as attending his school. After lessons, he could be kept inside with more work or the children. His letters to the man who arranged his support carried his despair. People treated that despair as a problem in his character. The arrangement continued. He also wrote a poem and read it during a visit away.

When he returned, the headmaster demanded to see it. He brought it, trembling. The man read it and dismissed it as sentimental rubbish. Writing had not made the house easier to live in. The headmaster's anger had become another part of it.

### 5. turning point

He remembered taking leave of the headmaster. Another teacher had described his treatment to the man arranging his support. That man had removed him from the school and the household. He thanked the headmaster for the kindness he had received. The headmaster answered with curses.

He said the young man would never become a university student. His poems, the headmaster predicted, would remain unsold. His body shook. He left the headmaster.

### 6. became

He rented a small room under a roof and began private lessons. The money for living also had to cover his teacher. He saved on other expenses, and families gave him meals. Twice a day he walked the long distance to his lessons. He studied languages and argued with a teacher who treated him kindly. An unfriendly face could still spoil a whole day. He had not left that fear behind with the school. He made comic versions of poems he had once written in tears.

Only a few poems reached paper alongside the schoolwork. He also tried to get a poem into print. Editors refused work from an unknown schoolboy. When it was finally published, an apology appeared beside it. The following year, he passed into university.

### 1. scene

He remembered taking a medical journal to bed. He was twenty-eight, with a new medical practice and debts he could not stop thinking about. Earlier that evening, he had prepared a class about how the body uses sugar. He read for hours and put notes on cards.

The lecture was ready before he got into bed. He had been looking forward to the journal that arrived that day. Now he read an article about a small gland inside the body. It described what happened when the gland's outlet became blocked.

### 2. dark moment

He lay awake after reading. The lecture and the article kept returning to his mind. So did the money he owed. He wanted to get out of debt and away from the worry. He had opened his practice only a few months earlier.

There had been long hours with little work. Expenses continued while very little money came in. The house and the car had put him deeper in debt. He lay with the accounts running through his mind. He could not put them away long enough to sleep. A class was prepared, but he was still awake in bed.

### 3. response

At about two in the morning, he got up and wrote down an idea. He named the disease at the top of a notebook page. He wanted to test whether an extract could reduce sugar in the urine. Below it, he set out an experiment.

He proposed separating the part of the gland that made digestive juices from the part making another substance. Then he could try to collect that substance and test its effect on the disease. The page contained a plan, not a result. He wrote down what to try. He spent much of the remaining night thinking about it.

### 4. struggle

He took the idea to a local colleague and then to a professor with a laboratory. The professor explained that other researchers had tried and failed. He also agreed that the experiment was worth trying and offered laboratory space. The young doctor stayed with his teaching work until the term ended. While waiting, he looked for another job. He applied to be a doctor on an expedition. He spoke to the organizer, then watched for the mail for weeks.

A letter finally came. The expedition was not taking a doctor. His hope of earning money there was gone. By spring, he left his practice and went to the laboratory. A student began working beside him. They operated on dogs and measured sugar in their blood. The work continued through the summer.

### 5. turning point

In the laboratory, he and his assistant prepared a liquid from a dog's gland. They had waited weeks after blocking its outlet, letting part of the gland waste away. They kept the preparation cold. Another dog had developed the disease after its gland was removed. They gave that dog an injection of the liquid.

Then they measured the sugar in its blood. The level had fallen. It was a positive result from the experiment he had proposed in his notebook. They still had to test what it could do. They continued their experiments with the extract.

### 6. became

The doctor still owed money and could not borrow more. He asked for pay, a room, help with the animals, and repairs to the laboratory floor. Dogs had died of infections. The work became a larger collaboration. A chemist worked on getting the active substance into a form suitable for people. Hospital doctors tested the preparations.

The first injection into a fourteen-year-old boy brought no clear improvement in his condition. His blood sugar fell a little, but that was not enough. Later injections used the chemist's preparation. The boy became more active and said he felt stronger. He needed repeated treatment. When injections stopped for a stretch, large amounts of sugar returned to his urine. The doctors resumed the injections.

### 7. bridge

His name was Frederick Grant Banting.

He helped develop insulin, a treatment that lets people with diabetes control their blood sugar.

### 1. scene

She was thirty, writing to the people printing her book. The bill had grown. She sent the extra money for paper and printing with her letter. The poems were hers and her sisters'. On the cover, none of them would use her own name.

She wanted copies sent to reviewers. If a notice appeared, good or bad, she wanted to read it. She could not regularly get those papers where she lived. Someone would have to send them.

She set out what she could afford to do next. If the notices were good, she would spend more on advertisements. If nobody noticed the book, more advertisements seemed useless to her. She thought the authors' names would not attract readers. She asked for the reviews anyway.

### 2. dark moment

Later that summer, she was away from home with her father. He could no longer read. He could make out familiar people when they stood against the light. They had come for an operation on his eyes.

She had stayed in the room throughout the operation. Her father had asked her to be there. She neither spoke nor moved until it was over.

On the day of the operation, her novel came back. She later remembered the two things happening together. The publisher had refused it. The answer was curt. It was the first novel she had been trying to place.

### 3. response

The next day, she wrote to a friend. Her father was in bed in a dark room. He was not to be moved for several days. He was to speak, and be spoken to, as little as possible.

She thanked her friend for advice about managing their stay. Most of what she had arranged already matched it. The agreement reassured her.

She had been obliged to trust a nurse with some things. She hoped the doctor would soon let her manage without the nurse.

The surgeon considered the operation successful. Her father could not see yet.

### 4. struggle

During that stay, she also began another novel. It was only at its beginning when they went home.

Writing did not arrive every day. Sometimes she went weeks without adding to a story, or longer. The new novel took shape while the old one kept coming back.

The poems had been out for a year. Only two copies had sold. She put that number in a letter to a writer she admired. She offered him a copy of what she could not sell. His books had given her pleasure.

Her sisters' novels found a publisher, on terms hard on their pockets. Hers still had no one. She sent it out again in its old wrapping. Earlier publishers' addresses had been crossed through, but remained readable. The next publisher could see where it had already been.

She waited, then wrote to ask whether it had arrived. She enclosed a cover addressed for the reply.

### 5. turning point

The reply came. She opened it expecting another short refusal. There were two pages. She remembered trembling as she read.

The publisher still would not take this book. But the letter discussed what worked in it and what did not. There were reasons, not just a dismissal. It offered to look carefully at a longer novel from the same writer.

The refusal cheered her. Her nearly finished second novel was already there to offer.

### 6. became

She finished the new novel and sent it. Even that took a small practical exchange. She could not pay the delivery charge where she left the parcel. She asked the publisher to tell her the cost so she could repay it.

A few weeks later, six printed copies reached her. She noticed the paper, the clear print and the way the books were bound. She wrote to thank the publisher for them. Then she waited for readers.

Some early reviews were cool. She worried that more criticism would hurt the book's sales. Getting into print had not stopped her worrying about what happened next. Her book was in print.

### 7. bridge

Her name was Charlotte Brontë.

She wrote Jane Eyre.

### 7. bridge

Her name was Julia Child.

She became a cookbook author and television teacher who made French cooking familiar to American home cooks.

At thirty-eight, she went to the travel agent and cancelled the tickets she had already booked. She asked for a different route. The previous evening, at dinner, she had sat beside a man recently returned from a distant city. He and his wife had described it with enthusiasm.

She had assumed she would have to travel there by sea. They had told her she could go by train. Now she booked tickets and reservations for the new route. The old holiday plan was gone.

The visas would take several days to arrange. There were preparations to finish before she could leave.

### 6. became

She was excited, but reluctant to go alone. She wanted to discover whether she could manage without depending entirely on other people. Five days after changing her plans, she left.

In the shared train compartment, another passenger wanted to take charge of her arrangements. The woman offered her a place to stay and urged her to abandon parts of her route. She thanked her, but kept her plans unsettled. They parted on friendly terms when the other passenger got off.

She continued. Outside were mountain gorges and people waiting on station platforms. She met other passengers and talked with them along the way. There was company without a fixed companion making every choice.

She was travelling on the route she had chosen. The book had been finished; the marriage had ended. She enjoyed watching from the train.

### 7. bridge

Her name was Agatha Christie.

She became a novelist and playwright known for stories that asked readers to puzzle out a mystery.
