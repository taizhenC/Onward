## Irving Berlin

[Candidate JSON](berlin_i-1901-1907-bowery-to-first-song-v1.candidate.json) · SHA-256 `4c733e4c4e971a2ed3a5412ee7d6ecf600554c247761321768b74f0ecf0b3898`

### Scene

He was a singing waiter, about nineteen, working in a café. Across the street, another waiter and pianist had written a hit.

He and the pianist at his own café began a song together. They wanted a song of their own. He wrote the words, and his partner wrote the tune.

It was a collaboration.

### Dark moment

The song was finished, but neither man could put its music on paper. The pianist could read a little sheet music. Writing down their own tune was different.

Their song still needed a written score.

### Response

The pair asked a shoemaker who also played the violin. He could not write down their music either.

They had found another player, but not someone who could make the score. The tune still needed to be written.

### Struggle

Eventually, a different violinist put the tune on paper. Neither collaborator had been able to do that. The first person they asked had not been able to do it either.

Now there was a score.

### Turning point

The publisher accepted their song. It was his first published song.

The printed sheet gave separate credit for the words and the music. The two collaborators had made different parts of it.

His part was the words.

### Became

The song did not become a hit. He remembered being surprised by its small earnings. It brought less than a dollar.

He remained a waiter. This first publication had not yet given him a living from songs.

The edition carried an initial and a surname. His collaborator was credited too.

His words were in print.

### Bridge

His name was Irving Berlin.

He wrote songs for singers, plays, and films.

Your life is not theirs.

But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You do not have to turn what you can do into a complete answer about who you are.

You are allowed to need other people.

## Charlotte Brontë

[Candidate JSON](bronte_c-1846-1847-two-copies-v1.candidate.json) · SHA-256 `62d167d5c3fff7acbd070b14e59c5eeb4f2386f63550c7e5a8cb18e47c8362a3`

### Scene

She was thirty-one, writing to an author whose books had given her pleasure. Her own book had been out for a year. It contained poems by her and her sisters. Only two copies had sold. She put that number in the letter.

The names on the cover did not disclose that the poets were women. She and her sisters had chosen them because they expected prejudice against women who wrote. She offered the author a copy in thanks for his writing.

### Dark moment

Her first novel still had no publisher. She remembered making the next submission with little hope. The refusals had offered neither a place for the book nor acknowledgment that it had merit. Despair had begun to reach her.

Her sisters' novels had been accepted, although the terms were poor. Her manuscript was going out again in old brown paper. The addresses of earlier publishers had been crossed out. They were still readable.

### Response

She wrote a letter to the new publisher. She asked whether the firm approved of the novel and could undertake to publish it soon. The request was brief. She gave the address for an answer.

It directed the publisher to her pen name, in care of her real name at home. It was the name already printed on the poems. She sent the manuscript with the letter.

### Struggle

More than two weeks passed without an answer. She wrote again. Her letter asked whether the manuscript had arrived and whether the firm wished to publish it. She enclosed a cover already addressed for their reply.

Another novel was also in progress. Writing had not always come steadily. She remembered stretches of weeks, sometimes months, without adding anything to a story. The new book had begun while she was away caring for her father. It was only at its beginning when they returned home. Now it was nearly complete. The old manuscript was still the one awaiting this publisher's answer.

### Turning point

The reply came. She opened it expecting another short refusal. There were two pages. She remembered trembling as she read.

The publisher was still refusing this book. But the letter discussed its strengths and weaknesses. It explained the business reasons for declining it. The writer offered to look carefully at a longer novel from the same author. She felt cheered by a letter that still said no. The second novel was already nearly finished.

### Became

She replied that she had another novel almost ready. She still proposed publishing the first book, followed by the second. She asked the publisher to consider that plan. Later that month, she finished the new manuscript and sent it. She could not prepay the delivery charge where she left the parcel. She asked for the cost so she could repay it.

That autumn, six printed copies reached her. She wrote to thank the publisher. She liked the paper, the clear print and the binding. Then she waited for the judgments of the press and the public.

Some early notices were cool. She worried that more criticism could hurt demand. Getting into print had not ended her concern about readers. The new book was in print.

### Bridge

Her name was Charlotte Brontë.

She wrote Jane Eyre.

Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You are allowed to care about work that has not found its readers. You do not have to call it worthless.

## Octavia Butler

[Candidate JSON](butler-1974-1975-pre-patternmaster-v1.candidate.json) · SHA-256 `925b5d1ca44ad0595efe29a80f6f35c7d1a26c088a093fe9e905b3c119e6a36e`

### Scene

She was twenty-seven when she lost her job. She cried.

Before the layoff, she had fitted writing around paid work. She got up in the early morning to write, then left for the office, factory or warehouse. She left jobs she hated and found others.

She had sold two stories years earlier. Only one had appeared. Nothing else had sold since.

### Dark moment

Unemployment payments were supporting her while she wrote. She was afraid that nothing would come of the writing.

Her relatives thought she should get a steady government job. She feared they might be right.

She had been trying to write a novel from an idea she first had at twelve. Having an idea had come long before knowing how to make a book. She was working, and still afraid.

### Response

A woman in the novel was meant to have only a small part. But her role kept growing as the book went on.

About halfway through, the writer could no longer leave that character as she was. She went back and tried to reorganize the story. She wanted the woman to seem to have belonged there from the beginning.

Her first plan had changed.

### Struggle

Two of her novels went to different publishers. While both were under consideration, she began revising an older book. She had started that one in her teens.

Her writing had earned money before. At eighteen, she had won money in a college contest. Later, two writers teaching a workshop bought stories from her. Only one appeared.

Five years followed without another sale. She was revising again.

### Turning point

During those revisions, her two submissions received different answers. One was accepted; the other was refused.

The accepted novel was the one in which the woman's role had grown. The other manuscript was still without a publisher.

One book was going into print.

### Became

The book came out the next year. After it was published, she took her first vacation. She bought a bus pass that let her travel for a month. She went to cities she had not visited before.

In one city, she met another woman who was sightseeing. They climbed a tall building together. The woman's shoes hurt. The writer was out of shape. They encouraged each other on the way up. She remembered that they made it to the top.

For once, the time away from a job was a holiday. There was a month to travel.

### Bridge

Her name was Octavia Butler.

She wrote science-fiction novels.

Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You can care about something without feeling certain it will work. You can still be unsure.

## George Washington Carver

[Candidate JSON](carver-1885-1891-turned-away-to-the-sod-house-v1.candidate.json) · SHA-256 `0d48e0817701019845e856457947aba7f1408b4f2e9bafff03241d7020d154a9`

### Scene

He went to arrange his meals at the college. He was about twenty-six. He had transferred there to study agriculture.

A professor had given him work caring for greenhouses and sent him to arrange a place in the dining hall. He was already a student. But he was told that he could not eat with the white students.

He was Black. He was to eat apart from them.

### Dark moment

He wanted to remain at the college. There was feeling against him because of his race, and he did not know what to do.

He had come to study agriculture. The dining hall was part of the college, but he was not allowed to share it with the white students. His meals were with hired workers in a basement room.

He wanted to stay in school. He needed advice.

### Response

He wrote to a friend from his previous college. He told her about the hostility he had met and how much he wanted to stay. He asked what he should do.

This was someone who had helped him before. She and her family had supported him during his earlier studies and helped him reach the new college.

He put the difficulty into a letter. He asked her for advice.

### Struggle

Admission had not settled the conditions of his daily life. Finding a room had been difficult. His former art teacher had asked a professor to find him work. He had been given a cleaning job and a former office to live in.

There had been help with work and a room. There was still the refusal to let him eat with the white students. The college had not become a place where he was treated like them at meals.

His letter reached his friend. She took a train to the college. Before seeing him, she spoke to his former art teacher.

### Turning point

He walked around the campus with his friend. She had travelled there after receiving his letter about the hostility he had met.

She stayed with him until evening.

### Became

He later told her that things had become easier from the following day. That was his account of the visit's aftermath. It was not the end of his need to earn a living while studying.

He remained a student, with paid work alongside his studies. The room and the work had helped him attend. When he needed advice about being treated differently, he had written to someone who knew him.

He still worked for his living.

### Bridge

His name was George Washington Carver.

He became a scientist who studied agriculture and taught others.

Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You may need help without losing your part in your own life. You are allowed to need people.

## Ray Charles

[Candidate JSON](charles_r-1945-1948-orphaned-to-the-bus-v1.candidate.json) · SHA-256 `41b662b6160575e8e691d624a071793f94004c5433fa1eb52dfd53aab01f822b`

### Scene

He remembered the message being read to him at boarding school. He was fourteen. His mother had died. A neighbor had sent the news, and he heard it the following morning.

He had been blind since childhood. His mother had taught him ordinary work: washing clothes, scrubbing floors and chopping wood. At school, his fingers had learned to read Braille. Music lessons meant memorizing notes he could not follow with his eyes. He went home for his mother's funeral.

### Dark moment

He could not eat. He could not cry. He felt the grief caught in his throat.

He loved his mother, and could not accept that she was gone. Her death was a fact he could not take in.

### Response

He returned to school that autumn, then left a few weeks later. He was fifteen when he went to live in a larger town. There, he agreed to stay with family friends and contribute money from playing. They fed him and cared for him.

Music was not only a way to earn. Playing gave him pleasure and a way to express himself. He wanted experience playing with other musicians. He had somewhere to stay.

### Struggle

He worked with small bands in several towns. Some days, a tin of fish, crackers and water were all he had to eat. He remembered thinking he could stay on the road unless someone convinced him he lacked the ability.

The bands borrowed the sound of musicians who were already popular. In one group, he and the other players copied songs and arrangements from a successful bandleader. They hoped that sounding like him could earn them money.

Eventually, he chose a city across the country. He wanted somewhere far away, but not a huge city. He thought he might get a chance there. He took the bus.

### Turning point

At the club door, he pleaded for admission. He was seventeen. He sang a blues song. The crowd responded warmly. People from other clubs were listening.

### Became

Two days later, he had a job. He worked as a singer and pianist in the new city. A younger musician began visiting his apartment. The visitor remembered finding him repairing his record player.

He could also explain how the music fitted together. He taught the visitor about notes and arranging. They worked through the lengths of notes and how they added up. The visitor struggled with the lesson, and kept at it.

For paying customers, he sang in the style of a popular singer and pianist. He hoped the familiar sound could help him keep working. He was also sharing what he knew with another musician.

### Bridge

His name was Ray Charles.

He became a singer and pianist whose music joined gospel and blues.

Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You are allowed to need other people while making a choice of your own. You do not have to feel certain about everything.

## Agatha Christie

[Candidate JSON](christie-1926-1928-the-year-that-broke-v1.candidate.json) · SHA-256 `ae8444b6a80550f4d8a3688faf8644083a1fb316d2b745fa65912d0c8ddd2cbd`

### Scene

She remembered trying to work on a novel with her secretary in a hotel garden. She was thirty-six. The secretary had a pencil ready. Her daughter was also staying at the hotel.

She had brought them away with her while she was separated from her husband. Her mother had died the previous year. Now she needed to write another book and earn money.

She told her daughter that the next hour was work time. She and the secretary were going to be busy. The child went away.

### Dark moment

She looked at her secretary and struggled to think of the words. The plot was already worked out. Part of it came from a story she had written before. She knew where the story was going, but she could not see its scenes. The characters did not feel alive.

There was no pleasure in the work. She needed the money another book could bring. Having a plot and needing an income did not make the writing come easily. She was trying to begin.

### Response

She began to dictate, hesitantly. After a few minutes she noticed her daughter across the path. The child wanted to know whether half an hour had passed. Only nine minutes had gone by.

She sent her daughter away and resumed the dictation. Then the child came back and asked to stand nearby without interrupting. She agreed, unwillingly, and started again.

### Struggle

With her daughter watching, the words sounded even more foolish to her. She faltered, hesitated and repeated herself. Starting again had not made the dictation easy.

The plot was in place, but the people in it would not come alive. She knew where the story was going. That did not bring pleasure to the writing.

She wrote most of the novel during the trip. She still disliked the book.

### Turning point

She completed the novel and sent it to her publishers. It was the book she had been struggling to dictate. She had got it written without coming to like it.

The book was finished.

### Became

It appeared the following year. Its sales were comparable to those of her previous book. She had needed the money another novel could bring, and the book had found readers.

That did not make her proud of it. She continued to dislike the novel she had finished. The book was in print.

### Bridge

Her name was Agatha Christie.

She became a novelist and playwright known for stories that asked readers to puzzle out a mystery.

Your life is not theirs.
But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You are allowed to have mixed feelings about something you have managed to finish.
You do not owe it a cheerful ending.
