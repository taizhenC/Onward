## Bessie Coleman

[Evidence-mapped candidate](coleman-1918-1921-no-school-would-take-her-v1.candidate.json) · 482 words · episode 1920-11-01–1921-10-08 · ages 28–29

SHA-256: `f819cc6b7cb22e4ea59f7eddebccfc46abc5d85958e679e6ed9781285f365434`

### 1. Scene

She was at a flying school abroad, signing a contract. She was about twenty-nine. In her account, the paper made her responsible for the risk to her life. The school did not take responsibility for her life. She signed. She had come from another country to learn to fly. Before the crossing, she had taken language classes and saved money. Other people had helped pay for the trip. Now the training required her own agreement to its risks.

### 2. Dark moment

At the school, she saw another student killed. The death shocked her nerves. She had signed a paper accepting danger to herself. Now someone learning the same work had died. The danger belonged to the lessons she had come to take. She was still a student there.

### 3. Response

She continued her training. She said that her nerve held and she kept going. Her course still involved going to school. She walked there as part of the work of learning. She wanted to finish the course.

### 4. Struggle

Getting into this school had already taken more than one attempt. The first school she approached abroad had refused to accept women. It had lost two women who were learning to fly. She had gone to a different school. Rejection had also shaped the route that brought her overseas. In her sister's recollection, schools at home refused her because she was Black. Some also refused her because she was a woman. She took the problem to a newspaper publisher who helped her. On his advice, she enrolled in a language school. He also helped her contact a school abroad. After all that, there were still lessons and the walk to school.

### 5. Turning point

She took the school's examination and passed. She had completed the training that followed the signed contract. A pilot's licence was issued to her. The paper recorded that she had qualified. Passing did not mark the end of her lessons. She kept flying to improve. Then she went to another school for further instruction. In her remembered account, the teacher was an experienced pilot. She was still taking lessons.

### 6. Became

She returned home as a licensed pilot. A reporter visited her there to ask about flying. She explained the training and the contract. She also spoke about people who had not had her chance. She wanted more Black people to be able to fly. Her own licence had not completed that work. She offered to share information about aviation and its schools. The article told interested people they could see her. She could answer questions about the course. Looking back, she said: “I tried and was successful.”

### 7. Bridge

Her name was Bessie Coleman.

She was the first African American woman to earn a pilot's licence. Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you. You are allowed to want something without knowing the whole way toward it. You do not have to feel fearless.

## John Coltrane

### 1. Scene

He was in a pianist's home, trying to find a tune on his saxophone. He was about thirty. In his account of their rehearsals, the pianist got out of bed to play. The pianist began one of his tunes and looked at him. He took his horn out and tried to follow. They played the music again, working through what he could not yet find. When a passage gave him too much trouble, the pianist brought out the written music. There was the part he needed to learn.

### 2. Dark moment

Earlier that spring, he had lost his place in another band. The bandleader had hired him two years before. Heroin use had made him unreliable at the job. Now the bandleader had dismissed him. In the bandleader's account, it was the second dismissal. He had lost that place the previous autumn too. This time, another saxophone player was brought into the band. The group carried on with someone else playing his part. His job there was gone.

### 3. Response

He told the pianist that he wanted to learn a tune. They had recorded it together, and he liked the music. The pianist invited him over. That request led to the visits with his horn. He asked about the things in the music that troubled him. The pianist answered at the keyboard. He could watch how the answer was played, then work with the sound himself. They went through the tune again until most of it was worked out. The written notes were there for difficult parts.

### 4. Struggle

The rehearsals led into a run of club performances. Playing with the pianist could mean playing without him for a while. He left the keyboard during the music. The saxophonist remembered feeling lonesome when that happened. He relied on the bass player to keep his place. But the bass player did not always play the obvious notes. Knowing the tune mattered. He had to follow another musician's choices while making his own. He knew the tune they were playing. The pianist's absence was part of that work. There were still bass and drums beside the horn.

### 5. Turning point

Later that summer, he brought a band into a recording studio in a living room. This time he led the session. Beside his saxophone were a trumpet and a trombone. Piano, bass and drums completed the group. They recorded tunes he had written, along with an older song. On the title piece, his saxophone solo came before the other horns. The trumpet followed him, then the trombone. The recorded music moved from one player to the next. More than the finished album survived the session. There were alternate performances, a false start, and a take left incomplete. A finished recording came from the work in that room. His own tunes were on it.

### 6. Became

He also kept playing with the pianist's group. The former bandleader came to hear him. In that man's memory, he was playing well and arriving reliably. The same account says that he had stopped using heroin. After his time with the pianist, he returned to his old bandleader. He was again playing as a member of someone else's group. Looking back at his own album, he said: “It was a good recording.” He liked the band on it.

### 7. Bridge

His name was John Coltrane.

He was a jazz saxophonist who wrote music for his bands.

Your life is not theirs.

But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You are allowed to be unfinished without making your whole life a verdict.

You are allowed to need help.

## Michael Faraday

### 1. Scene

Far from home, he was writing to a friend, his journal unfinished. He was twenty-two. Other work left little time for letters or for sorting his papers. A departure was close, and his notes were behind. He had come as a scientist's assistant, travelling far from home.

There were languages to learn and experiments to see. But the trip had not felt as he had expected. He had already thought seriously about going home. He stayed for the chance to learn more. For now, he had a letter to answer.

### 2. Dark moment

Later that autumn, he was writing from another city. No letters from home had reached him there. He missed the people who knew him, and the ordinary pleasure of their company. Earlier letters could have given him their words again. But he had left those letters behind with his books. He blamed himself for putting them so far away. He feared that he might never see them again. Then the fear reached beyond the missing pages. He could imagine never getting home. He had left people he loved to gain knowledge. Now he doubted whether that exchange had been wise. The old letters were out of reach.

### 3. Response

He wrote to his friend about the missing letters. In the same letter, he considered a less painful explanation. The post was slow and uncertain. He did not believe his friend's feelings had changed. He worked through the problem of paying for the letter. Advice about the route made him wary of paying everything in advance. He was sorry about leaving postage costs for his friend. Then he asked after his friend's health and sent love to both families. He meant to write to other friends too. He wanted to receive their writing as well.

### 4. Struggle

The winter brought small tasks as well as missing people. He explained his language study in a letter to his younger sister. He wrote unfamiliar words and their meanings on a sheet. The words were mixed together, with no neat separation between the languages. In the evening he checked them against his dictionary. A word he had learned got a line through it. The list shrank, then grew again with new words. Finding books could also fail. He searched several cities for a dictionary in the languages he needed. He ended with a dictionary in three languages instead. Buying books at every stop had made his collection too large. He had to limit the places where he bought them. His letters still contained doubts about how little he knew.

### 5. Turning point

By late winter, he could write a different report to his friend. He still had duties he had agreed to take only temporarily. A promised replacement for the scientist's personal servant had never arrived. But the scientist had tried to keep unwanted tasks away from him. Local servants now dealt with the scientist's wife's requests. Their quarrels had become less difficult. The scientist was away looking for somewhere to stay. The assistant had time to himself. He could visit the city, write his journal, and learn another language. He put the change into his letter: “now I am somewhat comfortable”. There was still a journal to write.

### 6. Became

In spring, he was on his way home. He sent his mother a letter about the approaching return. He wanted his first moments back to be with her. She could tell some close friends that he was coming. There were others he wanted to tell himself. The former employer who had cared for him was among them. Back at home, he resumed work as a laboratory assistant. The tour had ended; he still held an assistant's position. He had not returned as the head of a laboratory. There was work for him there.

### 7. Bridge

His name was Michael Faraday.

His experiments helped explain how to generate electricity and make electric motors. Your life is not theirs. But a piece of this story may still sit beside you. You are allowed to miss what you left behind without having an answer for everything that comes next. You do not have to feel settled yet.

### 2. Dark moment

She remembered being unable to open her mouth. Speaking in public frightened her. Even with her own children before her, this was unfamiliar. The task was a greeting at lunch, not a long speech. She had never addressed people in public. Now she had to speak.

### 3. Response

She practiced saying the Christmas greeting to her children. It was the speech she had been asked to give at the company lunch. Her children were her audience.

### 4. Struggle

### 1. Scene

### 2. Dark moment

One day, his mother came into the laundry crying. She said she could no longer stay with their relatives. Someone there had hurt her pride and her feelings.
