### 1. Scene

A letter addressed to her warned that delay could cost her the patent. She was twenty-seven. Her lawyers needed a signed form and payment. An earlier notice had allowed six claims in the application. That notice had not yet given her a patent. The final fee still had to be paid within the stated time. Another rule now had to be met before that payment. There was little time.

### 2. Dark moment

The fee was due within a month. The new form had to reach the government well before the fee. A certified copy of the application was needed too. Her foreign nationality was the reason her lawyers gave for these extra steps. They asked for her immediate attention. The letter offered a way to have her signature witnessed at their office. It also made the consequence plain. Delay could lose the patent.

### 3. Response

She signed the form. She also supplied the money for the final government fee. Her lawyers acknowledged her check. A letter to the man helping her recorded that he had sent the form by special messenger. The lawyers forwarded the signed form to their associates. They asked for the certified copy to be obtained quickly. These were completed steps, but the application was not yet finished. Her signature needed another check.

### 4. Struggle

A telegram reported a problem with her name. The name she had signed did not match the one on the application. Another form would have to be signed. Her lawyers sent it through the man helping her. This was not the first part of the application that needed changing. Months earlier, one claim had been rejected because of earlier patents. Six other claims had been allowed. Her lawyers had explained two ways to proceed. The rejected claim could be dropped. They could also propose another claim for the apparatus. The first application had needed revision.

### 5. Turning point

The new form was signed with the matching name. Her lawyers wrote that they were sending it to their associates. The particular defect in the earlier signature had been corrected. Other timing details still needed attention. Later letters moved the planned fee payment by one day. The earlier proposed date would not leave the full required interval. The papers still had to be handled in the right order. The new signature was in place.

### 6. Became

The patent was issued later that summer. It carried her name and her collaborator's name. It contained six claims. The system was meant to make interference with radio communication harder. Her lawyers had also asked that issuance be delayed. Their request was for a limited period after payment. The document eventually issued. Both inventors were named.

### 7. Bridge

Her name was Hedy Lamarr.

She was an actor who also helped invent a radio communication system.

## C. S. Lewis

### 1. Scene

At his notebook, he wrote about a fluttering stomach. He was sixty-one, and his wife had died. His stomach fluttered, although he did not think he was afraid. He described yawning and being unable to settle. People speaking to him seemed hard to hear through his grief. He wanted them near him. He wanted them to speak to each other. The thought of an empty house frightened him. Even a letter could ask more effort than he could give. His work still ran much as before, but shaving no longer seemed worth the trouble. He wrote about swallowing.

### 2. Dark moment

He wrote about the silence he met when he prayed. In urgent need, he felt no answer. He had tried to explain these thoughts to someone that afternoon. The other man had recalled the suffering of the central figure in their faith. He questioned whether that made his distress easier to understand. The fear he recorded was not chiefly that his belief would disappear. He feared that the God he believed in might be cruel. He had begged for reassurance that his wife still existed. He described receiving none. Even reassurance might now seem like something his own prayers had made him imagine. He could still form the questions. He could not hear an answer.

### 3. Response

He put a thought into the notebook. Then he put down another part of what he was living through. He could not record all of it. He thought the writing gave him a little room outside the pain. Reading had not given him enough of that distance. He questioned the writing even as he returned to it. Perhaps it only kept his mind circling the same loss. The grief was there, and the thought of being in grief was there too. He set down that doubt along with the rest. The notebook held both.

### 4. Struggle

When he first read the notes back, he disliked what he found. They made his wife's death seem chiefly a loss to him. He tried to think more about her. That did not settle things. His mind chose which words and looks to remember. He feared that his wife was becoming someone made by those choices. She could no longer interrupt him and be herself. He met a man he had not seen in years. The real man's presence exposed the gaps in his remembered version. The meeting sharpened his fear about remembering his wife. He had also tried to talk to the children about her. Their embarrassment stopped the talk.

### 5. Turning point

One early morning, he felt lighter. The sun was out after dull, damp weather. There was a small breeze. He had slept more soundly after a tiring, healthy day. He thought his body might be recovering from exhaustion. He did not make that the whole explanation. In that less painful moment, his wife came clearly to mind. The sudden impression was so vivid that ordinary memory seemed a poor name for it. Yet he stopped short of calling it a meeting. He wrote about what had happened. He could see how easily he might have misread another grieving person. Less visible sorrow could have looked like forgetting. His experience that morning had been different. For that moment, he remembered her clearly.

### 6. Became

That was not where the grief ended. In a later entry, his plan to remember her with gladness had failed. The bitterness, the fluttering stomach, and the tears came back. He kept writing until he reached the last nearly empty notebook in the house. Old sums filled some pages at the back. He resolved to let this book set the limit. He would not buy another for these notes. Writing had helped, but it had not made grief stand still. There were gains he was unwilling to call lasting. There were walks that hurt in a new way. The last book still had those old sums.

### 7. Bridge

His name was Clive Staples Lewis.

He wrote the children's fantasy books called The Chronicles of Narnia.

Your life is not theirs.

But a piece of this story may still sit beside you.

You do not have to make every part of an experience agree.

You are allowed to be unfinished.

### 1. Scene

A young student remained in the house where she had been accused of poisoning two fellow students. She was about seventeen. The other women had fallen ill after leaving the house for an outing. One had said that she had been poisoned. The accusation had come back to the place where all three boarded.

Her lawyer remembered the waiting that followed. The sick women had not recovered. She and the people supporting her waited for their condition to change. They hoped they would live.

### 2. Dark moment

The accusation remained against her while the two women lay ill. She was a Black student in a household shared with white students. ### 1. Scene

A young doctor went to see a colleague at his hospital about a rejection letter. He was thirty-one. He and another doctor had been studying bacteria found in patients' tissue samples. They thought the bacteria might help explain an illness they treated. They had submitted their work to a meeting of specialists. The meeting was being held in their own city. That mattered when travel cost more than they could easily afford. But the letter said their work had not been accepted. He brought the problem to a colleague.

### 2. Dark moment

He remembered the rejection as the hardest day of the dispute. The letter itself was polite. It thanked him for sending the work. It said that many papers had been submitted and most had been accepted. His was among those left out. It described a committee choosing without knowing who had written each paper. It suggested that he might submit again after colleagues had examined the work. Those were possibilities for another submission. This meeting had turned him down. The page ended with a formal sign-off. There was no place for his talk.

### 3. Response

He went to the doctor who was supervising him. The man worked with bacteria. He had been growing these bacteria too. He could see that they were there. The young doctor had reached someone with direct experience of the work. In his memory, that was who he approached after the letter arrived. He had gone to another person.

### 4. Struggle

Before that letter, the work had included long stretches of failure. They had seen the bacteria in tissue but had struggled to grow them. The early attempts had produced no culture they could study. Eventually, they succeeded. They also collected information from patients undergoing examination. Bacteria appeared alongside inflammation and ulcers in the results. That association was a reason to investigate. It did not, by itself, establish what caused what. Meanwhile, the young doctor still had patients and training duties. He fitted the research around them. He took the references with him when his job moved him far away. There he worked through the results and prepared them to be presented. The rejected submission had come after that work.

### 5. Turning point

In his later account, his colleague answered with encouragement. The colleague was growing the bacteria too. He could see that they were real. He thought the work had a future. This was support from someone examining the same problem. It was not proof that every claim was right. He had heard an encouraging answer.

### 6. Became

His later autobiography describes the arrangements that followed. His colleague supplied a contact. Another researcher arranged a presentation at a European meeting. A travel grant helped make the trip possible. He traveled to the meeting. During that trip, another group also found the bacteria in a patient's sample. More people were working on the question. But the following year still brought rejected papers and experiments that failed. He also carried out a risky experiment on himself and became ill. The result added evidence that the bacteria could inflame the stomach. It did not establish that he had given himself an ulcer. He recovered from the acute illness.
